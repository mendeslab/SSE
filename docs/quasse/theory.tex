This selective reference records mathematical reasoning that can be consulted and extended during
development. It derives the trait generator, fossil-aware branch and node calculations, the local
reaction flow, and root/origin conditioning. It does not provide a complete error analysis of the
numerical likelihood. The \href{guide.pdf}{guide} gives an introductory account of the model.

The argument follows the pruning calculation: define its conditional quantities, derive how
they change along branches, combine them at observations, and finally integrate over the starting
trait and impose the chosen sampling condition. Section~\ref{sec:reaction} explains numerical
propagation; it can be skipped on a first reading of the model likelihood.

\section{Conventions and conditional quantities}\label{sec:conventions}
The latent trait is $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Forward elapsed time is $s$; age before the present is $t$.
Birth and death rates are $\lambda(x)$ and $\mu(x)$. Fossil sampling is non-removing with scalar
rate $\psi\geq0$, and present sampling has scalar probability $\rho\in[0,1]$. Sampling events
and trait observations are distinct: $g(y\mid x)$ is the density of measured trait $y$ conditional
on latent trait $x$. The rates $\lambda,\mu,\psi$ have units of inverse time; $\rho$ is a
dimensionless probability. In this document, all trait integrals are over the real line unless
a finite numerical domain is explicitly specified.

Conditional on a single lineage at age $t$ with trait $x$, define $E(x,t)$ as the probability of no
sampled descendants between that time and the present, including no fossil samples. Consequently
$0\leq E\leq1$ and $E(x,0)=1-\rho$. Define $D(x,t)$, for a specified observed descendant subtree,
as its likelihood contribution conditional on that lineage and trait, before any whole-tree root
conditioning. This contribution integrates over unobserved events and trait histories. It is
nonnegative but need not be at most one: it is a density in the observed event ages and trait
measurements. Different observed subtrees have different $D$ functions; the subtree is suppressed
in the notation. By contrast, $E$ depends only on the model and on $(x,t)$.
The node and starting-condition sections specify the event-density and conditioning conventions.

Increasing $t$ means moving toward the past in the pruning calculation. It does not reverse
the biological process: a lineage at age $t+\delta$ evolves \emph{forward} for duration $\delta$
to age $t$. Its trait at the older end is the argument $x$ of the backward likelihood.
Subscripts $E_t$ and $E_x$ denote partial derivatives, not different probabilities.

These meanings and the living-tip boundary $D(x,0)=\rho g(y\mid x)$ follow the conventions described
in the \href{../../examples/QuaSSE_fossils_fixed_tree.md}{package fossil guide}. They concern the
unscaled mathematical quantities: numerical normalization of $D$ does not change its definition,
and $E$ is a probability rather than a freely rescalable likelihood.

\section{Brownian trait generator}\label{sec:generator}
Assume constant drift $v\in\mathbb{R}$ and constant variance rate $q>0$, on the unbounded trait line.
The forward process, conditional on $X_0=x$, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:trait-process}
 X_s=x+vs+\sqrt{q}\,W_s,
\end{equation}
where $W_s$ is standard Brownian motion. Drift has units trait/time and $q$ has units
trait$^2$/time. In the package, the input named \texttt{diffusion} is $q$.

For a smooth function $f$, define the backward transition operator
$P_s f(x)=\expect[f(X_s)\mid X_0=x]$. It averages a contribution at the younger end of a
branch over the possible descendant traits, conditional on the older trait $x$. The term
\emph{backward} refers to its action on functions of that starting trait; $X_s$ itself evolves
forward. For the following elementary derivation, it suffices to take
$f$ three times continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives. For a small interval
$\delta>0$, write $Z=v\delta+\sqrt{q\delta}\,\xi$ with $\xi$ standard normal. Taylor expansion gives
\begin{align*}
 \expect[f(x+Z)]&=f(x)+f'(x)\expect[Z]
                   +\tfrac12 f''(x)\expect[Z^2]+O(\expect[|Z|^3]),\\
 \expect[Z]&=v\delta,\qquad
 \expect[Z^2]=q\delta+v^2\delta^2,\qquad
 \expect[|Z|^3]=O(\delta^{3/2}).
\end{align*}
Subtracting $f(x)$, dividing by $\delta$, and taking the limit proves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generator}
 \traitop f:=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\frac{P_\delta f-f}{\delta}
       =v f_x+\frac q2 f_{xx}.
\end{equation}
This is an infinitesimal identity under the stated assumptions, not a numerical error bound.

\section{Backward likelihood transport and forward densities}
For trait-only propagation of a descendant contribution $f$, let $u(x,t)=P_t f(x)$. Extending the
ancestral-to-descendant interval increases $t$. The Markov property gives
$u(\cdot,t+\delta)=P_\delta u(\cdot,t)$. Thus the difference quotient in $t$ is exactly
the generator limit applied to the current function $u(\cdot,t)$, yielding
\begin{equation}\label{eq:backward-transport}
 u_t=\traitop u=v u_x+\frac q2 u_{xx},\qquad u(x,0)=f(x).
\end{equation}
For an already positive-aged descendant, $t$ in this trait-only formula may be replaced by the
elapsed branch duration. The time-homogeneous operator is unchanged. Full likelihood propagation
also includes diversification and sampling terms, derived in Section~\ref{sec:fossil-likelihood}.

By contrast, the forward density $p(x,s)$ of the evolving trait obeys the adjoint equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward-density}
 p_s=-v p_x+\frac q2 p_{xx}.
\end{equation}
For a smooth test function $f$ and vanishing boundary terms,
\[
 \frac{d}{ds}\int p f\,dx=\int p\traitop f\,dx
 =\int f\left(-v p_x+\frac q2 p_{xx}\right)dx.
\]
The first equality follows by averaging the infinitesimal change in $f(X_s)$; integration by
parts gives the second. Since it holds for all such $f$, it identifies the forward-density
equation. A density tracks where traits go; a backward likelihood asks how the starting trait
affects future observations. These different questions account for the different drift signs.

As a check, take $f(x)$ to be a Gaussian profile with center $m$ and variance $a>0$. Then $P_t f$
has center $m-vt$ and variance $a+qt$, with the same integrated weight. A forward Gaussian density
instead moves its center to $m+vs$. The former convention is used in the
\href{../../validation/log_pde/README.md}{log-space transport experiments}.

The unbounded-domain calculation does not justify any particular finite-grid boundary rule.
Spatial discretization, time integration, and boundary truncation require their own analysis.
The original QuaSSE model is described by FitzJohn~\cite{fitzjohn2010}; the derivation here states
its constant-coefficient trait convention explicitly rather than attributing fossil extensions to
that paper.

\section{Fossil-aware likelihood pruning}\label{sec:fossil-likelihood}
Sampling at rate $\psi$ records the lineage without removing it or changing its latent trait.
Both daughters inherit the parental trait at birth; their subsequent histories are independent
conditional on that trait. Measurements are conditionally independent given the traits at their
observation events. Rates are finite locally and sufficiently regular for the backward generator
calculation; the following short-interval expansions are infinitesimal statements.

\subsection{Between observed events}
Extend a lineage from age $t$ to age $t+\delta$, with initial trait $x$ at the older end.
For the event of leaving no samples, the possibilities in this short interval are no discrete
event, death, or birth followed by two unsampled daughter histories. A fossil observation is
incompatible with no samples. The no-event contribution averages over the younger trait using $P_\delta$:

\[
 E(x,t+\delta)
 = [1-(\lambda+\mu+\psi)\delta]P_\delta[E(\cdot,t)](x)
   +\mu\delta+\lambda\delta E(x,t)^2+o(\delta).
\]
The three displayed terms represent no event, death, and birth, respectively. Death contributes
$\mu\delta$ times one because a dead lineage has no future samples. Birth contributes
$\lambda\delta E^2$ because both daughters must leave no samples. Rates without arguments are
evaluated at $x$. Terms involving two or more discrete events are included in $o(\delta)$.
Trait changes in the already order-$\delta$ event terms affect only the remainder. Subtracting
$E(x,t)$, dividing by $\delta$, and taking the limit gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E}
 E_t=\traitop E+\mu-(\lambda+\mu+\psi)E+\lambda E^2,
 \qquad E(x,0)=1-\rho.
\end{equation}
The boundary means that a living lineage at the present is not sampled with probability $1-\rho$.

For a specified nonempty observed subtree, consider an interval containing no observed node.
A death or sampling event on the continuing lineage cannot be the sole event in this short
interval: death removes the observed continuation, and sampling introduces another observation. Birth is possible if exactly one daughter supplies
the specified subtree and the other leaves no samples. Either daughter can be the continuing
one, so the two alternatives contribute $DE+ED$. A birth with observed descendants on both
sides would instead be a node of the observed tree, excluded from this interval. Therefore,
\[
 D(x,t+\delta)
 = [1-(\lambda+\mu+\psi)\delta]P_\delta[D(\cdot,t)](x)
   +2\lambda\delta E(x,t)D(x,t)+o(\delta).
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
 D_t=\traitop D+[2\lambda E-(\lambda+\mu+\psi)]D.
\end{equation}
The term $-\psi D$ excludes additional fossil observations on that interval. It does not mean
that sampling kills a lineage. Both equations integrate over unobserved biological histories;
observed events are applied separately at their known ages.

\subsection{Calculations at observed nodes}\label{sec:node-events}
For each node, propagate any observed descendant branches to its age $t$ before combining them.
Write $D^+$ for the contribution immediately ancestral to the event, with no extra elapsed time.
The plus sign refers to the older side, in the direction of increasing $t$; it is not a time
derivative. The descendant $D$ values in the table are evaluated just before applying the event.
Every factor in Table~\ref{tab:node-events} uses the same trait $x$ at the event.

A living tip combines its sampling probability with its measurement density. At a fossil age,
$\psi\,dt$ is the sampling probability to first order, so $\psi$ is the event-time density factor,
not a probability to be multiplied by the solver's timestep. For a terminal fossil, the
non-removing sample is followed by an unobserved continuation. Summing over all continuations
with no later samples gives $E(x,t)$, including biological survival without further sampling.
Immediate death is neither required nor imposed. A sampled ancestor instead has an observed
continuation, with contribution $D_{\rm continuing}$; multiplying by another $E$ would incorrectly
require that continuation to leave no samples.

At a bifurcation, the birth-time density and conditional independence of the two daughter
histories give the displayed product. The package uses a fixed daughter-order convention with
one factor $\lambda$ per observed split; it does not sum over exchanging the displayed daughters.
This differs from the two hidden-birth alternatives in Equation~\eqref{eq:D}, where either
daughter may leave no samples. Likelihoods defined on differently ordered or labelled tree
spaces can require combinatorial factors; those are not additional branch-PDE terms.

A sampled ancestor is not an ordinary bifurcation and contributes no $\lambda$.
In BEAST's tree representation, the observation is a zero-length leaf attached to its
continuing lineage by a \emph{fake} parent. Together they represent one sampling event; the
virtual observation leaf does not supply a second lineage or a separate branch integration.

Unlike $D$, $E$ does not acquire an observation factor or a product of daughter values at an
observed node. It remains the probability for a hypothetical single lineage with the given
trait and age to leave no samples. In particular, the $\lambda E^2$ in its differential
equation already integrates over possible births in unobserved histories; an observed split
does not add another such update to $E$.

\subsection{How branches carry the no-sample probability}\label{sec:branch-E}
Mathematically there is one function $E(x,t)$ for the given rates and present-sampling boundary.
It is independent of the specified observed subtree. The implementation nevertheless propagates
an $E$ array separately alongside $D$ on each branch, rather than reading from a shared
time-indexed trajectory. Thus $E$ is available at the same integration times and reaction stages
used to advance $D$, without temporal interpolation from another calculation.

A living tip starts with $E(x,0)=1-\rho$. For each terminal fossil, an $E$-only calculation
starts at the present and advances to the fossil's age; that array supplies both the terminal
factor in Table~\ref{tab:node-events} and the starting $E$ for its ancestral branch.
At an ordinary bifurcation, the implementation copies the left child's $E$ at the node age;
at a sampled ancestor it copies the continuing child's $E$. Internal branches therefore
inherit an array, rather than restarting from the present. Origin-stem propagation similarly
advances the root's $E$ and $D$ together.

This approach repeats some work. Distinct branch histories can partition time differently,
so their numerical arrays at the same age can differ slightly even though they approximate
the same mathematical $E(x,t)$. Copying one child's array is an implementation choice, not
conditioning $E$ on that child's observations. Synchronizing $E$ with $D$ avoids a separate
interpolation approximation but does not make either propagation exact.

\section{Reaction flow and numerical branch propagation}\label{sec:reaction}
At a fixed trait bin, suppress trait transport and hold $\lambda,\mu,\psi$ constant. The local
reaction equations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reaction}
 \dot E=\mu-(\lambda+\mu+\psi)E+\lambda E^2=:F(E),
 \qquad \dot D=F'(E)D.
\end{equation}
Dots here refer to elapsed backward duration within a reaction step. These equations have an
exact local flow, even though their composition with trait transport is approximate.

\subsection{Solving the reaction equation}
Let $E_0,D_0$ denote the values entering a reaction step, and $E_h,D_h$ its values after
backward duration $h\geq0$. Assume nonnegative rates and $0\leq E_0\leq1$.
The quadratic $F(E)$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:reaction} has discriminant
\[
 \kappa^2=(\lambda+\mu+\psi)^2-4\lambda\mu
          =(\lambda-\mu-\psi)^2+4\lambda\psi.
\]
Define its nonnegative square root $\kappa$ and the quantities
\[
 \beta=\frac{\lambda+\mu+\psi+\kappa}{2},\qquad
 e_*=\begin{cases}\mu/\beta,&\beta>0,\\0,&\beta=0.\end{cases}
\]
For $\lambda>0$, the equilibria are $e_*$ and $\beta/\lambda$. Writing the lower one as
$\mu/\beta$ also handles $\lambda=0$ without dividing by $\lambda$.
When all rates vanish, $\beta=0$ and the choice $e_*=0$ simply gives the identity flow.
In every case,
\[
 F(e_*)=0,\qquad 2\lambda e_*-(\lambda+\mu+\psi)=-\kappa.
\]

Shifting to $y=E-e_*$ therefore removes the constant term:
$\dot y=-\kappa y+\lambda y^2$. For $y\ne0$, set $z=1/y$. Then
$\dot z=\kappa z-\lambda$. Multiplying by $e^{-\kappa h}$ and integrating gives
\[
 e^{-\kappa h}z_h=z_0-\lambda I_h,\qquad
 I_h=\int_0^h e^{-\kappa u}\,du
 =\begin{cases}(1-e^{-\kappa h})/\kappa,&\kappa>0,\\h,&\kappa=0.\end{cases}
\]
Inverting and writing $w=e^{-\kappa h}$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shifted-reaction}
 E_h=e_*+\frac{(E_0-e_*)w}{1-\lambda(E_0-e_*)I_h}.
\end{equation}
The formula extends continuously to $E_0=e_*$, where the flow stays at equilibrium.

For evaluation, the implementation rewrites the denominator using
$\beta-\lambda e_*=\kappa$ and $w+\kappa I_h=1$. Put
\[
 A=\beta-\lambda E_0,\qquad
 B=1-\lambda(E_0-e_*)I_h=w+A I_h.
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:shifted-reaction} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reaction-update}
 \boxed{\quad E_h=\frac{wE_0+e_* A I_h}{B},\qquad
               D_h=D_0\frac{w}{B^2}.\quad}
\end{equation}
To justify the $D$ update, differentiate Equation~\eqref{eq:shifted-reaction} with respect to
its initial value: $J_h=\partial E_h/\partial E_0=w/B^2$.
Differentiating $\dot E=F(E)$ with respect to that same initial value gives
$\dot J=F'(E)J$, $J_0=1$. This is the $D$ equation with initial value one, so its solution
with initial value $D_0$ is $D_0J_h$. Both updates must use the same old $E_0$.

The evaluated denominator is positive in exact arithmetic: $\kappa\geq
|\lambda-\mu-\psi|$ implies $\beta\geq\lambda$, hence $A\geq0$ and $B=w+A I_h>0$
for finite $h$. To avoid cancellation, the implementation uses
$I_h=-\operatorname{expm1}(-\kappa h)/\kappa$ when $\kappa>0$, where
$\operatorname{expm1}(u)=e^u-1$ is evaluated accurately near zero.
It also writes $A=\eta+\lambda(1-E_0)$ with $\eta=\beta-\lambda$.
For $a=\lambda-\mu-\psi>0$, the identity
\[
 \eta=\frac{\kappa-a}{2}=\frac{2\lambda\psi}{\kappa+a}
\]
avoids subtracting nearly equal rates. These rearrangements evaluate the same exact flow.

\subsection{Limiting cases and interpretation}
At $h=0$ the update is the identity. With $\lambda=0$ and $c=\mu+\psi>0$, it reduces to
\[
 E_h=\frac{\mu}{c}+\left(E_0-\frac{\mu}{c}\right)e^{-ch},
 \qquad D_h=D_0e^{-ch}.
\]
When all rates vanish, $E$ and $D$ are unchanged. With $\psi=0$, the equations and update reduce
to the birth--death reaction. In its critical case $\lambda=\mu=b$, $\kappa=0$ and
\[
 E_h=1-\frac{1-E_0}{1+b(1-E_0)h},
 \qquad D_h=\frac{D_0}{[1+b(1-E_0)h]^2}.
\]
If $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are trait-independent, the spatially constant initial condition for
$E$ stays constant on the unbounded trait domain: $\traitop E=0$. Its full PDE then reduces to
this reaction ODE. The observed-data function $D$ can still depend on $x$ through trait
measurements, so constant diversification links do not generally eliminate trait transport.

\subsection{Splitting and scales}
A branch step of length $\Delta t$ applies a reaction step of length $\Delta t/2$, trait
transport for $\Delta t$, and another reaction step of length $\Delta t/2$, advancing $E$ and
$D$ together. This is Strang splitting. For smooth vector fields $R$ (reaction) and $T$
(transport), Taylor expansion of their exact half/full/half flows gives, with $U=(E,D)$
and $V=R+T$,
\[
 U_{\rm step}=U+hV(U)+\tfrac12 h^2 V'(U)V(U)+O(h^3),\qquad h=\Delta t.
\]
Here $V'(U)V(U)$ means the derivative of the vector field $V$ in the direction $V(U)$.
The exact combined flow has the same expansion: $U'=V(U)$ and $U''=V'(U)V(U)$.
Thus one step differs first at order $h^3$. Under regularity and stability
assumptions that keep these remainder estimates uniform, the accumulated splitting error over a
fixed interval is of order $h^2$. For a PDE this argument requires control of the relevant
operator derivatives on its solution. It does not bound the complete implemented solver:
finite grids, sampled Gaussian kernels, boundary treatment, and resolution transfer add errors.
A finer timestep alone need not improve a spatially underresolved kernel.

For fossil-enabled calculations, each branch segment is divided into an integer number of
steps that reach its endpoint. A branch crossing the resolution-switch age is split there,
and both arrays are transferred from the fine grid to the coarse grid. Events exactly at
the switch age use the coarse grid.

The mathematical $D$ in Table~\ref{tab:node-events} is unscaled. Numerically, scalar sampling
factors $\rho$ and $\psi$ are stored as logarithms, and branch normalizations of $D$ are
accumulated and restored in the final log likelihood. This prevents those rescalings from
changing the likelihood. $E$ is a probability and is never freely rescaled.
Section~\ref{sec:conditioning} gives the final trait integration and conditioning.

\section{Sampling success and starting conditions}\label{sec:conditioning}
The quantities $E$ and $D$ retain their meanings and equations from
Section~\ref{sec:fossil-likelihood}. Let $Q(x,t)$ be the probability of no sampled
\emph{living} descendants from a lineage at age $t$ and trait $x$. It satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q}
 Q_t=\traitop Q+\mu-(\lambda+\mu)Q+\lambda Q^2,\qquad Q(x,0)=1-\rho.
\end{equation}
To see why fossil sampling drops out, consider a short forward interval. A fossil observation
leaves the lineage alive and does not alter the event of having no sampled living descendants.
Its contribution $\psi\,\delta Q$ cancels the $-\psi\,\delta Q$ term from the probability of
no event. Death contributes $\mu\,\delta$, and birth contributes $\lambda\,\delta Q^2$.
Thus the existing $E$ solver can evaluate $Q$ with sampling rate zero, without changing
$\psi$ in the observed-data calculation, Equations~\eqref{eq:E}--\eqref{eq:D}.
The sample-success choices follow the biological distinctions in the BEAST sampled-ancestors
inputs~\cite{saInputs}; SSE retains its historical root/any-sample default.

\subsection{A single-lineage origin}
The age of the oldest observed node is the sampled root age $t_r$. It need not be the age
when the biological process started. Let $t_o\geq t_r$ be the origin age. Prune the observed tree and
propagate its root partial to $t_o$ to obtain $D_o(x)$. There is no birth or observation event at
the origin. For a normalized, data-independent starting-trait density $\pi_o$, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:origin-numerator}
 N_o=\int \pi_o(x)D_o(x)\,dx.
\end{equation}
Here $N_o$ is the observed-data density before conditioning on sampling success.
If $S$ is the required sampling-success event and the observed data satisfy $S$, conditional
probability gives $p(\text{data}\mid S)=p(\text{data})/P(S)$. Marginalizing over the trait yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:origin-condition}
 L_o=\frac{N_o}{Z_o},\qquad
 Z_o=\begin{cases}
 \int\pi_o(x)[1-E(x,t_o)]\,dx & \text{any sampled descendant},\\
 \int\pi_o(x)[1-Q(x,t_o)]\,dx & \text{sampled living descendant},\\
 1 & \text{no sample-success condition}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
These ratios require $Z_o>0$. If $Z_o=0$, the conditional model is undefined; if the observed
data do not satisfy $S$, their conditional likelihood is zero. Conditional probability here
concerns densities in the observations, not the probability of one exact continuous dataset.

The trait must also be conditioned on sampling success. Where $P(S\mid x)>0$,
\[
 \pi_o(x\mid S)=\frac{\pi_o(x)P(S\mid x)}{Z_o},\qquad
 p(\mathrm{data}\mid x,S)=\frac{D_o(x)}{P(S\mid x)}.
\]
Multiplying these two expressions and integrating cancels $P(S\mid x)$ and gives $N_o/Z_o$.
Regions with zero success probability have zero conditioned trait mass.
Averaging the pointwise ratio with the original $\pi_o$ would miss that change of weights.

The observed-event factors remain those in Table~\ref{tab:node-events}. Stem fossils are
therefore allowed, including a sampled-ancestor root; a stem does not add a hidden root event.

\subsection{Starting at a bifurcation}
Root conditioning starts with two daughters at an ordinary bifurcation of age $t_r$.
First separate their combined descendant contribution from the density of the split itself:
\[
 H_r(x)=D_{\rm left}(x,t_r)D_{\rm right}(x,t_r),\qquad
 D_r(x)=\lambda(x)H_r(x).
\]
The pruning calculation produces $D_r$. SSE retains a weighting density $\pi$ for this
quantity. Its convention corresponds to a starting-trait density at the split of
\[
 \pi_{\rm split}(x)=\frac{\pi(x)\lambda(x)}{C},
 \qquad C=\int\pi(x)\lambda(x)\,dx,\quad 0<C<\infty.
\]
Thus $\pi$ is a pre-split weight, not itself the trait density conditional on starting at a
bifurcation. The factor $\lambda$ favors trait values at which a split is more likely.
This is the package's retained convention; a separately specified trait density at a split
would be averaged against $H_r$ directly.

With normalized, data-independent $\pi$, the descendant-data density under this starting model is
\[
 \int\pi_{\rm split}(x)H_r(x)\,dx=\frac{N_r}{C},
 \qquad N_r=\int\pi(x)D_r(x)\,dx.
\]
For sample-success conditioning, both daughters must satisfy the chosen event. At fixed $x$
their futures are independent, so the success probability is $[1-E(x,t_r)]^2$ or
$[1-Q(x,t_r)]^2$. Integrating against $\pi_{\rm split}$ gives $Z_r/C$.
Dividing the data density by that success probability cancels $C$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:root-condition}
 L_r=\frac{N_r}{Z_r},\qquad
 Z_r=\begin{cases}
 \int\pi(x)\lambda(x)[1-E(x,t_r)]^2\,dx & \text{any sample on each side},\\
 \int\pi(x)\lambda(x)[1-Q(x,t_r)]^2\,dx & \text{living sample on each side},\\
 \int\pi(x)\lambda(x)\,dx & \text{no sample-success condition}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
As for the origin, the conditioned cases require a positive denominator and data satisfying
the selected event. Without a sample-success condition, $Z_r=C$ and $L_r=N_r/C$.
The common normalization of $\pi$ cancels in these ratios. This explains why retaining the legacy
root quadrature convention does not require renormalizing its Flat weights. Root/living conditioning
assigns zero support to a tree with an all-fossil daughter, even when the denominator is positive.
A sampled-ancestor root is not a starting bifurcation and is excluded in root mode.

For a constant positive $\lambda$, its explicit root factor cancels between numerator and
denominator. Birth rates still affect propagation on the descendant branches; they do not
disappear from the likelihood. With trait dependence, this convention does not identify $\pi$
itself as the trait density conditional on a starting split. Removing sample-success conditioning
still conditions on that split. In particular, this option differs from the raw birth-density
calculation obtained by disabling survival conditioning in diversitree.

When $t_o=t_r$, origin propagation is the identity and preserves all observed root factors. It
does not change the single-lineage starting model into a bifurcation-conditioned model; their
normalizations and support requirements still differ.

\subsection{Trait weights and numerical scales}
At the origin, useful-bin midpoint quadrature with spacing $\Delta x$ normalizes the Flat density
as $\pi_i=1/(n\Delta x)$, where $n$ is the number of useful bins. Write $D_i$ for the
endpoint partial at bin $i$. The compatibility option Observed instead uses
\begin{equation}\label{eq:observed-weights}
 \pi_i=\frac{D_i}{\sum_j D_j\Delta x}.
\end{equation}
Weights are formed after stem propagation at the endpoint resolution and used unchanged for both
integrals. A common positive scale in $D$ cancels in Equation~\eqref{eq:observed-weights}. However,
because these weights depend on the data, their substitution defines a comparison score, not the
conditional likelihood under an independently specified generative prior.

Flat refers to the finite useful trait interval, not a proper uniform prior on the real line.
Automatic grid bounds depend on observations, and padding can change useful-bin boundaries.
Thus Flat on an automatically selected interval should not be described as a wholly
data-independent scientific prior. Fixed-support comparisons and numerical domain checks are
needed to separate weighting conventions from discretization effects.

If $D=e^c\widetilde D$, where $\widetilde D$ is the final stored partial and $c$ is the
accumulated log scale, the reported log likelihood is
\[
 \log L=c+\log\int\pi(x)\widetilde D(x)\,dx-\log Z.
\]
Here $Z$ is the denominator for the selected starting and sampling conditions. Neither $E$ nor $Q$ is
rescaled as a likelihood. Their numerical calculation, like stem transport, uses the existing
spatial and temporal approximations; the conditional-probability identities above do not supply
a global numerical error bound.
